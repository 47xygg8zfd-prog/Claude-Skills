# UX Research Synthesis: PM Technical Upskilling

## What We Need to Learn

1. In which specific PM workflows does technical knowledge (or its absence) most affect the PM's effectiveness and confidence?
2. What does the learning experience feel like today — and where does it break down?
3. What would "just enough technical knowledge" look like from the PM's own perspective?

## Assumed User Profile

**Who**: Mid-level PM (2–5 years experience) at a B2B SaaS company with a dedicated engineering team of 5–15 people. Works in 2-week sprints. Has a CS degree or has worked in tech for several years but has no formal software engineering background.

**Their current behavior**: Googles unfamiliar terms in real-time during meetings. Asks their tech lead to explain things after the fact. Relies heavily on one or two trusted engineers to translate technical decisions. Avoids challenging estimates because they don't have a framework for evaluating them.

**Their stated goal**: "I want to be more technical so I can be a better PM and get taken more seriously by engineers."

**Their underlying goal**: They want to feel confident and credible in engineering conversations — not to become an engineer, but to never feel like the least informed person in the room.

## Assumed Pain Points (ranked)

| Pain | Frequency | Intensity | Evidence / Source |
|------|-----------|-----------|------------------|
| Can't evaluate whether an engineering estimate is reasonable | Weekly (every sprint) | High | Assumption — confirmed direction from discovery brief |
| Doesn't know what questions to ask in architecture reviews | Weekly | High | Common PM feedback in community forums |
| Feels embarrassed asking "basic" technical questions in group settings | Recurring | High | Discovery brief: "outsider" framing |
| Can't read a pull request or understand what changed | Per-release | Medium | Assumption — varies by team culture |
| Struggles to write technical requirements that engineers find useful | Per-feature | Medium | Common PM complaint |
| Can't explain technical trade-offs to non-technical stakeholders | Occasional | Medium | Assumption |

## Jobs to Be Done

| When... | I want to... | So I can... |
|---------|-------------|------------|
| I'm in a sprint planning meeting and an engineer gives an estimate I don't understand | Quickly calibrate whether that estimate sounds reasonable | Push back credibly or accept with confidence |
| I'm reviewing a technical design doc before sign-off | Know which questions are worth asking vs. which are implementation details | Not waste engineers' time and not sign off on something I don't understand |
| I'm writing acceptance criteria for a complex feature | Use the right technical vocabulary | Avoid back-and-forth where engineers have to translate my requirements |
| I'm preparing for a promotion conversation | Show that I operate at a technical level that matches senior PM expectations | Get promoted |
| An engineer explains something I don't understand | Learn it in context without having to schedule a follow-up or admit I'm lost | Stay in the flow of the meeting |

## Key Insights for the PRD

1. **The gap is context-specific, not general**: PMs don't need to learn Python. They need to learn *what questions to ask when an engineer says "we need to refactor the data model"*. Any product that teaches general programming will miss the market.

2. **Psychological safety is the real barrier**: Multiple pain points trace back to embarrassment — PMs avoid asking questions, avoid pushing back, avoid writing detailed technical requirements because they fear looking unintelligent. The product must make learning feel private and low-stakes, not performative.

3. **The moment of need is synchronous**: PMs most need help during a meeting, during a design review, during a sprint planning session — not the night before. A "study at home" product misses the use case. The product needs to work in the 60 seconds before a meeting or during async review.

## Risks If We Skip Research

We'll build a Duolingo-for-engineers and miss the real job to be done (contextual, workflow-specific confidence). We'll design for the stated desire ("learn to code") instead of the underlying need (feel credible in engineering conversations). Churn will be high because the product won't connect to day-to-day PM work.

## Recommended Research (if time allows)

1. **5 PM discovery interviews** (1.5 hrs total): Focus on critical incidents — "tell me about the last time a technical gap caused a real problem." Estimated time: 2 weeks including recruiting. Highest ROI research available.
2. **3 EM interviews** (45 min total): "What does a technically fluent PM do differently? What would make PMs easier to work with?" Validates the distribution hypothesis and surfaces the employer-buyer angle.
